# Tabernacle: architecture, gathering, and visual implications

**DRAFT — NOT FOR PUBLICATION. Research for creative review, not an official parish theological statement.**

The useful starting point is **a dwelling made coherent by joined parts**, rather than a stock church building. The biblical account offers textiles, connected construction, a distinct interior, a court and threshold, movement, and communal response. These can inform the logic of a new mark without producing a miniature reconstruction or an illustrated sermon.

This note is grounded in the literary descriptions below. It does **not** establish an archaeological reconstruction, historical date, or the parish's doctrinal interpretation. It also does not approve a logo, palette, typeface, or slogan.

| Textual observation | Design opportunity | Boundary on the claim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exodus 25:8 says the sanctuary is to be made so that God may dwell **among** the people. | Give the whole form a shared interior or a clear relationship among its parts. A belonging story can emerge from the construction itself. | Do not imply that a graphic contains God, that the parish owns divine presence, or that its logo is a sacred object. |
| Exodus 26:1–6 describes woven curtains coupled together; verse 6 describes the tabernacle as a unit. | Joining and continuity are unusually relevant formal ideas: many pieces become one dwelling. A seam or interdependence can be an ownable detail. | The exact counts and ancient fittings are not required in the logo. Assigning a theological meaning to an arbitrary number of graphic parts would be invented symbolism. |
| Exodus 26:7,14–16,26–30 describes additional coverings and an upright timber-and-bar structure. | Layer, fold, supported enclosure, and robust proportions offer an architectural vocabulary beyond an arch or roof. | A modern folded shape is an abstraction inspired by the text; it is not a verified depiction of ancient fabric drape or construction. Material names also vary by translation. |
| Exodus 26:31–37 distinguishes the inner division and the entrance screen. Exodus 27:9–19 describes a surrounding court, its hangings, supports, and gate. | Interior and exterior, threshold, positive mass, and deliberate negative space can carry the concept. The court is described rectilinearly; a dome or semicircular arch is not a necessary tabernacle sign. | A Bible text is not a surviving architectural survey. Do not label an abstract mark “the biblical floor plan,” convert its proportions into sacred geometry, or call a modern doorway historically exact. |
| Numbers 4:5–15,29–32 describes covering, carrying, and assigned transport duties. Exodus 40:36–38 links the community's journeys with the cloud. | Portable belonging, a structure assembled through contribution, and continuity through movement are meaningful alternatives to permanent masonry. | Do not present a modern mobility theme as the church's official mission, or imply that the church is transient or without a home. |
| Exodus 40:34–35 describes cloud covering the tent and glory filling it; Moses cannot enter at that moment. | A protected central interval can suggest attention, wonder, or presence without drawing a human figure or claiming to depict God. | A glowing aperture, sunburst, light beam, or white center is a **modern visual interpretation**. These verses describe cloud and glory, not an instruction for a logo shaped like a sun or window. |
| Psalm 100:4 connects entering gates/courts with thanksgiving and praise. Psalm 150 includes instruments, dance, and everything that breathes. | Praise can be expressed as joyful entry, response, cadence, movement, or shared voice. It is wider than a harp or a musical department. | Psalm 100 is not a measured wilderness-tabernacle plan. Psalm 150's instrument list does not privilege the harp as the full identity of this parish. |
| Hebrews 9:1–12 discusses the earthly sanctuary, divided spaces and restricted ritual access, then presents its Christian theological argument about Christ. | Retain the seriousness of sacred space while expressing the parish's desired welcome through a contemporary identity. | Do not depict a crowd inside the Holy of Holies as if that were the account. Do not collapse Israel's tabernacle, later temple imagery, and a modern church auditorium into one historically identical building. |

## Description, history, and interpretation must stay separate

**Verified here:** the cited literary descriptions and the distinction between their described structures and modern church pictograms. The study can responsibly say “inspired by joined curtains,” “a contemporary threshold interpretation,” or “a shared center suggests gathering.”

**Not verified here:** the survival of particular tabernacle materials, a precise archaeological site, a historical date or authorship model, the exact appearance of all fittings, and any specific physical reconstruction. Academic and encyclopedia sites were blocked by the environment's proxy during retrieval. The failed attempts are recorded, not presented as sources that were read. A full historical or archaeological claim requires a separate verified scholarly review.

**Creative interpretation:** geometry, a bright or empty center, rising motion, fabric-like folds, rhythmic spacing, or a letter connection. These may be good design ideas; none becomes “the biblical symbol” merely because it has a plausible explanation. A museum model, devotional painting, contemporary tabernacle visitor site, or internet diagram would likewise be an interpretation to assess, rather than excavation evidence or an authoritative template to trace.

This distinction is useful creatively. It permits original abstraction while keeping the explanation honest.

## Implications for the six proposed territories

These are territories for **different construction ideas**, not six names for the same arch or a promise that every idea will survive sketching.

| Territory | Research-based formal question | What a successful idea would achieve | Specific risk to test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. The Dwelling of Praise** | Can one continuous or interdependent construction establish a dwelling and an outward response in the same gesture? Joined fabric provides a stronger starting point than a house plus an added instrument. | One stable whole with an expressive edge or interval; shelter and response share the same structure. | A closed seal can feel exclusive; adding flourishes afterwards creates two unrelated symbols. |
| **B. Enter His Courts** | Can a deliberate opening or change in spacing invite entry without drawing a generic arch, door, arrow, or stick figure? | An immediately intelligible, welcoming relationship between outside and inside, reinforced by the name. | A keyhole or security-company reading; implying that historical inner-sanctuary access was unrestricted. |
| **C. The Presence Within** | Can a purposeful central space organize the entire mark rather than act as leftover blank area? | Quiet focus, dignity, and a memorable counterform that works without gradients or a literal depiction of God. | An empty container, eclipse, camera aperture, or generic technology icon. White space must have a clear formal reason, not an elaborate theological rescue story. |
| **D. One House, Many Voices** | Can distinct contributions visibly depend on one another to form one dwelling or shared space? | Unity that preserves difference; the “many” and “one” are observable in the structure. | Generic community rosettes, linked hands, or persons under a roof. Do not assign the part count to the Trinity, tribes, ministries, or congregational demographics. |
| **E. A Dwelling that Sings** | Can an architectural mass itself develop cadence, repetition, or uplift while remaining a coherent enclosing form? | Praise communicated through the way the dwelling is shaped, without an appended harp, note, or equalizer. | Choir-only positioning; sound-wave or technology-brand resemblance; rhythm disappearing at avatar size. |
| **F. The Living Wordmark** | Can the actual name carry a shared-space, joining, or entering idea through custom letter relationships? | A recognizable, legible “Tabernacle of Praise” whose crafted typography does meaningful work without requiring a symbol. | A forced ligature, misread name, or hidden rebus that only works after explanation. The letters must still read before the symbolism is discussed. |

No one territory has priority because it is more literal. The strongest identity may be typographic or abstract if it is recognizable, legible, warm, and connected to the name without a long explanation.

## Practical evaluation rules

- **Meaning before decoration:** describe the formal premise in one sentence before drawing it. The dwelling and praise should explain the same structural decision.
- **Visible versus intended:** write separately what a viewer can see and what a designer hopes it suggests. A blank counter is visibly a counter; “divine presence” is an intended association, not a directly observed fact.
- **Open enough to welcome:** test whether enclosure feels protective or excluding, and whether the central space feels gathered or vacant.
- **Praise beyond music:** a useful non-instrumental mark can express thanksgiving, response, movement, or shared voice. Instruments remain optional and must earn their place.
- **Monochrome and scale:** the defining connection should survive at 24–32 px; inspect 16 px as a stress test. Do not let tiny seams carry the entire meaning.
- **Name and affiliation:** use the standalone words **Tabernacle of Praise**. Keep the authorized official RCCG emblem separate and unchanged; a labeled reservation is sufficient until the asset is supplied.
- **No invented doctrine or numerical symbolism:** no claims that a particular curve depicts God, that a part count represents a theological truth, or that these proposed creative themes are an official mission statement.
