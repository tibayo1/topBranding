# Scriptural foundations for the strategic exploration

**DRAFT — NOT FOR PUBLICATION. Research for design; no parish doctrine, mission statement, or identity is approved.**

Research date: **17 October 2026**. Quotations below use the **World English Bible (WEB)**. Their wording was checked against retrieved verse data, rather than recalled from memory. See [retrieval notes](sources/scriptural-retrieval-notes.md) and [selected verse evidence](sources/scriptural-excerpts.json). The latter also includes the architectural passages requested for the companion research.

## Strategic finding

The most useful intersection is **a dwelling among a people, made expressive through their response of praise**. This is a proposed design synthesis, not a quotation or an official statement of the parish's beliefs. It supports one integrated form whose spatial structure and expressive behavior belong together. It does not require a harp, a roof, a cross, a luminous center, or a literal reconstruction of a biblical building.

The texts offer relationships—among, within, entering, responding, together—before they offer a catalogue of objects. Those relationships can generate coherent construction rules. A logo still needs an immediately convincing silhouette; scriptural relevance cannot rescue arbitrary geometry.

## What the named passages actually say

| Passage and verified short quotation | Textual observation | Possible design implication | Contextual limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exodus 25:8:** “Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.” | The sanctuary is related to God's dwelling **among the people**. Verse 9 then refers to a prescribed pattern for the tabernacle and its furniture. | Explore a shared interior, a surrounding structure, interdependence, or a form made by several parts. Dwelling need not become a conventional house outline. | “Belonging” and “welcoming” are present-day creative themes, not words in this verse. A contemporary parish logo is not the divinely prescribed construction pattern. |
| **Exodus 40:34–35:** “Yahweh’s glory filled the tabernacle.” | A cloud covers the Tent of Meeting; glory fills it. Moses cannot enter while the cloud remains there. Verses 36–38 connect the cloud and fire with Israel's journeys. | Presence can be explored through the relationship between enclosure and an important interior, or through a structure that holds attention without filling every space. | Do not label a white void, circle, flame, or ray as a picture of God. “Light” is an artistic association here, not the wording of verses 34–35. This passage also resists a simplistic claim that every biblical sacred space was freely accessible to everyone. |
| **Psalm 100:4:** “Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise.” | Entering and the worshippers' response occur in the same sentence. The surrounding psalm includes gladness, singing, the plural “we,” God's goodness, and enduring faithfulness. | A threshold can be constructed by the movement of the same elements that form the dwelling. An opening may carry an expressive rhythm rather than have a music icon placed beside it. | The poem's gates and courts do not establish the dimensions, elevation, or exact appearance of the wilderness tabernacle. Do not treat a generic modern arch as a verified biblical gate. |
| **Psalm 150:1, 3–6:** “Praise God in his sanctuary!”; “Let everything that has breath praise Yah!” | Praise encompasses trumpet, harp and lyre, tambourine and dancing, stringed instruments and flute, and cymbals. The final invitation is broader than the instrument list. | Collective response, breath, articulation, and varied elements held in one order are stronger starting points than a mandatory instrument. A restrained mark can suggest joy through movement and proportion. | A harp is one legitimate possibility, not the defining or exclusive biblical image of praise. “Breath” is an interpretive prompt for form, not permission to portray the Holy Spirit as an invented swirl. |
| **Hebrews 9:1–12, 24:** “an earthly sanctuary”; “not made with hands”; “into heaven itself” | The chapter describes sanctuary divisions, objects, and restricted priestly access, then develops a contrast involving Christ's priesthood and the greater tabernacle. It is not merely an architectural inventory. | Favor an identifier that can suggest sacred dwelling without insisting that the church is its tent, its current venue, or a reproduction of an ancient floorplan. Layering or spatial depth may be useful if the simple silhouette survives. | A literal floorplan cannot carry the chapter's full Christian argument. Do not convert its typology into a purported parish doctrinal statement, or imply that the logo depicts heaven, Christ's work, or divine presence itself. |

## Implications for the six proposed territories

- **The Dwelling of Praise:** Make containment and expression arise from the same contour. Test whether praise changes the structure rather than being attached as an accessory.
- **Enter His Courts:** Let an intentional opening and the approach toward it provide the idea. It must move beyond the rejected doorway/arch vocabulary; the verse supplies a relationship, not a default icon.
- **The Presence Within:** Give the interior a meaningful role in the whole. Describe it as a visual association with sacred space, never as an image or containment of God. Avoid relying on a glowing dot to supply spirituality.
- **One House, Many Voices:** Let distinct elements establish one dwelling or shared space. Psalm 100's “we” and Psalm 150's breadth support collective-response research. The number of parts is compositional unless a separately justified meaning is established; do not add Trinity, apostle, or tribe symbolism after drawing.
- **A Dwelling that Sings:** Investigate cadence, repetition, tension, opening, and release in the architecture itself. This need not depict sound waves, strings, or an instrument. Avoid a mark that identifies only a choir.
- **The Living Wordmark:** Use the complete approved name to carry the identity. A distinctive relationship between letters may express gathering or response, but the words must remain readable without an explanation. Scripture does not prescribe a particular ligature.

## Guardrails for development and review

1. **Separate evidence from invention.** Label a measured arrangement as a schematic reading of the text, and an expressive shape as contemporary abstraction. Neither is an archaeological reconstruction by default.
2. **Keep the object of praise clear in the explanation.** These passages address worship of God. A form about people alone can become a generic community or cultural organization mark; the name, parish context, and separate authorized RCCG emblem must do their appropriate work.
3. **Do not equate sacredness with exclusion.** Biblical sanctuary access and modern parish welcome are distinct contexts. Explain a welcoming mark as a present-day design goal, not as a claim that everyone entered the Holy of Holies.
4. **Do not depict or domesticate God.** Cloud, fire, light, and empty space can become visual metaphors; no such graphic is a verified likeness of God or a guarantee of God's presence.
5. **Do not assign theological meaning to convenient geometry.** Three gaps do not automatically mean Trinity; twelve strokes do not automatically mean apostles; gold does not automatically mean divine glory. An unexplained composition may simply have a visual rhythm.
6. **Avoid biblical color determinism.** The tabernacle's textile colors and materials belong to its description. They do not require a permanent blue-purple-red-gold parish palette. First judge the new concepts in monochrome.
7. **Judge the shape before its story.** Each shortlisted mark should be memorable, readable at small size, and credible beside the full name before the scriptural connection is explained. The explanation should reveal the construction's relevance, not excuse it.
